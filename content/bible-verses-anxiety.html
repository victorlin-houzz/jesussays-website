Bible Verses for Anxiety and Worry

By the Jesus Says teamUpdated September 28, 2026Scripture: King James Version

The Bible's clearest verses for anxiety are Philippians 4:6–7 and 1 Peter 5:7: bring every worry to God in prayer, because he cares for you, and his peace will keep your heart and mind. Scripture never pretends worry is small. It gives you somewhere to put it, and words to use when your own run out.

Care note: Prayer and Scripture support care; they don't replace it. If anxiety is constant, comes with panic attacks, or keeps you from sleeping, eating, or working, talk with a doctor, counselor, or pastor. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) in the US, or contact local emergency services.

What does the Bible say about anxiety and worry?

The King James Version never uses the words “anxiety” or “worry.” It speaks of being “careful,” of taking “thought,” and of carrying “care,” which in 1611 English all meant being anxious. Read with that in mind, these six passages speak straight to a troubled mind.

  • Philippians 4:6–7 — “Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus.” Paul wrote this as a prisoner, so it isn't advice from an easy life. He doesn't promise the problem will vanish. He promises that God's peace will keep, or guard, your heart and mind while you wait.
  • 1 Peter 5:6–7 — “Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in due time: Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you.” Peter ties the two together: handing God your cares is how you humble yourself under his hand. It admits you can't carry everything, and it trusts that he wants to.
  • Matthew 6:34 — “Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof.” Jesus is honest that tomorrow will bring trouble (“evil” here means hardship). He asks you to carry one day at a time, the day God has actually given you.
  • Luke 10:41–42 — “Martha, Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things: But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part, which shall not be taken away from her.” Jesus says her name twice, gently. Martha's serving was good work. Her worry had simply pulled her away from the one thing she needed most, which was time with him.
  • Psalm 94:19 — “In the multitude of my thoughts within me thy comforts delight my soul.” The Hebrew word for “thoughts” suggests restless, divided thinking, which is why many modern translations say “anxieties” or “cares.” God's comfort meets the psalmist in the middle of the noise, before it stops.
  • Psalm 139:23–24 — “Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting.” It's the same Hebrew word for troubled thoughts. This is a prayer for when you can't sort out your own mind: you invite God to look at all of it and lead you on.

Is anxiety a sin?

Feeling anxious is not a sin. It's part of living in a body and a world that can hurt you, and the Bible's most faithful people felt it. David wrote psalms from caves and sleepless beds. Paul, who wrote “be careful for nothing,” also admitted that “the care of all the churches” came upon him daily (2 Corinthians 11:28). He even praised Timothy for caring about the Philippians (Philippians 2:20) with the same Greek verb he uses for worry in Philippians 4:6.

So concern itself isn't the problem. The question is what you do with it. Scripture's steady answer is to turn it into prayer, again and again, instead of carrying it alone or pretending it isn't there. And if worry has become a constant state instead of a passing wave, that isn't a spiritual failure either. It's a reason to get help, as you would for any burden that won't lift.

How do I pray Bible verses when I'm anxious?

You don't need a long quiet time. A few unhurried minutes, most days, is easier to keep than an hour once a month. Here is a simple pattern.

  1. Pick one passage ahead of time. Choose tonight what you'll read tomorrow, so you aren't hunting for a verse with a racing mind.
  2. Read it slowly, twice. The first time for sense. The second time, notice the phrase that catches, such as “he careth for you.” That phrase is your anchor for the day.
  3. Name the worry plainly. Not “I'm stressed,” but the actual thing: the test results, the conversation with your son, the rent. Philippians 4:6 says “in every thing,” so nothing is too small or too specific.
  4. Pray the verse back to God, with thanks. Put it in your own words and thank him for one real thing he has already done. The prayer below is one way to start.
  5. Return to your phrase during the day. Write it on a card or set a reminder. When the worry comes back, and it will, answer it with the same words.

If writing helps you, keep one line a day: the worry you named and the verse you prayed. Over a few weeks it becomes a record of how God met you, which is worth rereading the next time anxiety tells you he won't.

Father, you know what I'm carrying today. I bring it to you by name: ______. I cast this care on you, because you care for me. Thank you for the ways you've already carried me. I don't ask to see all of tomorrow. Give me what I need for today, and let your peace keep my heart and mind in Christ Jesus. Amen.

What if the anxiety doesn't lift after I pray?

That's common, and it doesn't mean the prayer failed. Many psalms end with the writer still waiting. Prayer is less like flipping a switch and more like walking back to the same safe place until the path is familiar. Keep going, and don't keep going alone: tell a friend, your pastor, or a small group what you're carrying, and let them pray with you.

It can also help to name what kind of anxiety this is. If it's closer to fear of something specific, read these Bible verses about peace and fear or what Jesus said to people who were afraid. If it keeps you awake, try this night prayer for sleep and anxiety.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best Bible verse for anxiety?

There isn't one right verse, but Philippians 4:6–7 is the one most people return to, because it gives both a practice (pray about everything, with thanks) and a promise (God's peace will keep your heart and mind). If you need something short enough to hold in one breath, try 1 Peter 5:7. At night, Psalm 94:19 fits a mind full of thoughts.

What does “Be careful for nothing” mean?

In the King James Version, “careful” means full of care, or anxious. So Philippians 4:6 isn't telling you to be careless. Modern translations render it “do not be anxious about anything.” Paul pairs the command with a practice, bringing every request to God with thanksgiving, and a promise that God's peace will guard your heart and mind through Christ Jesus.

Is worry the same as anxiety in the Bible?

The Bible doesn't draw clinical lines between them. The Greek word behind “careful” and “take thought” covers everyday worry as well as deep, lingering unease, and Scripture's response is the same for both: bring it to God. Anxiety that is constant or overwhelming may also call for a doctor or counselor, and seeking that help fits comfortably with faith.

Is it wrong for a Christian to take medication for anxiety?

No. Nothing in Scripture forbids medicine, and the Bible speaks warmly of physicians; Paul calls Luke “the beloved physician” (Colossians 4:14). Whether medication is right for you is a decision to make with your doctor. Taking it is not a lack of faith, any more than wearing glasses is. You can pray and receive treatment at the same time.

How long should a daily devotional for anxiety be?

Five to ten minutes is enough. Read one short passage slowly, name one specific worry, and close with a one-sentence prayer. Consistency matters more than length, and a short practice you actually keep will do more than a long one you dread. On hard days, a single verse read twice still counts.
