Is Anger a Sin? What the Bible Says About Anger

By the Jesus Says teamAI-assisted, reviewed by VictorUpdated September 29, 2026Scripture: King James Version

No, anger is not a sin in itself. Ephesians 4:26 says, "Be ye angry, and sin not," and Jesus was angry when he cleared the temple. Anger becomes sin when it turns into cruelty, revenge, or bitterness you keep feeding. James 1:19–20 says to be slow to wrath, because human anger doesn't produce the righteousness God wants.

Care note: Anger never justifies threats, intimidation, or violence, and forgiveness never requires you to stay in danger. If someone's anger is frightening or hurting you, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 (US) or local help. If your own anger feels out of control, talk with a pastor or licensed counselor. Prayer supports that care but doesn't replace it. In a crisis, call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US) or contact local emergency services.

Is anger a sin?

Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath: Neither give place to the devil.

Ephesians 4:26–27

Paul assumes believers will get angry. His command is about what comes next, and he gives three guardrails: don't let anger carry you into sin, don't let it sit overnight and harden, and don't give the devil a foothold through it. The emotion itself is not the problem. What we do once anger takes the wheel is.

Scripture shows God himself angry at evil while also describing him as slow to anger and plenteous in mercy (Psalm 103:8). That is the pattern held out to us: anger at what is truly wrong, held with restraint.

What does the Bible say about controlling anger?

Proverbs says the person who is slow to anger is better than the mighty (Proverbs 16:32). The New Testament gives the same counsel with more detail.

  • James 1:19–20 — "Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath: For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God." Notice the order: listen first, speak second, get angry last. Anger feels righteous in the heat of the moment, but it seldom brings about the good it promises.
  • Proverbs 15:1 — "A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger." A gentle reply is not weakness. It often lowers the temperature enough for the real issue to be dealt with.
  • Romans 12:19 — "Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord." To "give place unto wrath" means to leave room for God's judgment. You can call a wrong what it is and still refuse to take revenge.
  • Ephesians 4:31–32 — "Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you." Paul lists what anger becomes when it's fed: bitterness, shouting, slander, malice. Then he names the way out, which is kindness and forgiveness rooted in how God has forgiven us.

Did Jesus get angry?

Yes. In John 2:13–17, at Passover, Jesus made a whip of cords, drove out the animal sellers, overturned the money changers' tables, and said, "make not my Father's house an house of merchandise" (John 2:16). The other Gospels describe a similar scene in his final week (Matthew 21:12–13). In Mark 3:5 he looked around at religious leaders with anger, grieved by their hard hearts, because they cared more about a Sabbath rule than a man with a withered hand.

Look at what made him angry: worship turned into a marketplace, and a suffering person treated as less important than religious appearances. His anger defended God's honor and people being pushed aside. When he was insulted and beaten himself, he did not retaliate (1 Peter 2:23). That gives us a useful test. Anger at real wrong, aimed at making it right, can be righteous. Anger at being inconvenienced or embarrassed usually isn't, however justified it feels.

How do I handle anger without sinning?

  1. Pause before you act. Step away, take a walk, or wait until morning to send the reply you've drafted.
  2. Tell God the truth. The Psalms bring raw anger to God instead of hiding it. Honest prayer is far safer than bottled-up rage.
  3. Ask what the anger is pointing to. A real injustice? An old hurt? Fear, exhaustion, or an expectation no one knew you had?
  4. Speak honestly and calmly. Ephesians 4:25 calls for speaking truth. When it's safe, take the issue to the person involved rather than to everyone else.
  5. Release revenge. Hand the outcome to God (Romans 12:19). Forgiveness is often a decision you return to more than once. For help with that, see love and forgiveness.

When does anger become dangerous?

Anger that shows up as threats, intimidation, breaking things, controlling someone, or physical harm is not a private spiritual struggle to manage quietly. It is a safety issue. If you are on the receiving end, your safety comes first. Forgiving someone does not mean staying where you are in danger or pretending the harm didn't happen. Reach out to the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233 in the US), local emergency services, or a trusted pastor.

If you're the one whose anger is hurting people, admitting it is a good first step. Talk to a pastor and a licensed counselor, and consider an anger-management group. Repentance includes getting the help you need to change. Anger that lingers for months after a deep wound, or that is damaging your sleep and relationships, is also worth bringing to a counselor.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does "let not the sun go down upon your wrath" mean?

Paul's line in Ephesians 4:26 means don't let anger settle in and harden. He isn't setting a strict rule that every conflict must be resolved before bedtime; some conversations need more time or a safer setting. The point is to deal with anger promptly, through prayer, an honest conversation, or a choice to release it, before it grows into resentment.

What is righteous anger?

Righteous anger is anger at what God himself opposes, such as cruelty, injustice, abuse, or hypocrisy, and it moves a person to protect others and set things right. It isn't about wounded pride. Because almost everyone believes their own anger is justified, it helps to ask a trusted friend whether your response looks more like Jesus or more like revenge.

What did Jesus say about anger?

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus said that "whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment" (Matthew 5:22). He treated contempt and insults as serious, not only violence, and told listeners to make things right with someone they had wronged before offering a gift at the altar. Many modern translations leave out "without a cause," following older manuscripts.

Is it wrong to be angry at God?

Many faithful people in Scripture brought anger and complaint straight to God, including Job, Jeremiah, and several psalmists. God did not reject them for their honesty. Telling God what you really feel is better than pretending or quietly pulling away from him. Over time, honest prayer can move toward trust, as many lament psalms do by their final lines.

Can I forgive someone and still feel angry?

Yes. Forgiveness is a decision to give up revenge and release the debt someone owes you, and feelings often catch up slowly. You may need to forgive again each time the memory returns. Forgiveness also doesn't erase consequences or require you to trust someone again right away. You can forgive and still keep firm boundaries, especially with someone who has been unsafe.
