Bible Verses for Anxiety About Money

By the Jesus Says teamAI-assisted, reviewed by VictorUpdated September 29, 2026Scripture: King James Version

The best Bible verses for anxiety about money, such as Matthew 6:31–33 and Hebrews 13:5, don't promise wealth. They promise that your Father knows what you need and will not leave you. Scripture takes bills seriously: it invites you to pray specifically, plan wisely, ask for help, and trust God with the part you can't control.

Care note: This page offers Scripture and prayer, not financial, legal, or debt advice. For urgent decisions, talk with a qualified advisor or a nonprofit credit counselor, and let your pastor know what's happening; your church may be able to help. If money stress has left you feeling hopeless or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) in the US, or contact local emergency services.

What Bible verses help with anxiety about money?

  • Matthew 6:31–33 — “Therefore take no thought, saying, What shall we eat? or, What shall we drink? or, Wherewithal shall we be clothed? (For after all these things do the Gentiles seek:) for your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things. But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.” Jesus names the exact worries: food, drink, clothes. He doesn't call them silly. He says your Father already knows you need them. Seeking God's kingdom first puts money back in its place: important, but not in charge of you.
  • Hebrews 13:5 — “Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.” In the KJV, “conversation” means your way of life, as the Greek behind Hebrews 13:5 shows. The writer answers money fear with a promise of presence: whatever the balance says, you won't face it alone. Contentment grows from that promise more than from the numbers.
  • Philippians 4:11–13 — “Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me.” Verse 13 is often quoted as a promise of success. Read in context (Philippians 4:10–19), it's about money and circumstances: Paul had learned to live with plenty and with hunger, and Christ gave him strength for both. If you're in a lean season, it was written for exactly where you are.
  • Philippians 4:19 — “But my God shall supply all your need according to his riches in glory by Christ Jesus.” Paul wrote this to a church that had just given sacrificially to support him (Philippians 4:18). It's a promise that God meets needs, often through his people, and it isn't a formula for getting rich. Sometimes his provision arrives as a friend's help, a church's gift, or a door you didn't expect.
  • Proverbs 30:8–9 — “Remove far from me vanity and lies: give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: Lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the LORD? or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain.” This is one of the most honest prayers in the Bible. Agur asks for enough and admits that both wealth and poverty carry spiritual dangers. It's a good prayer when you don't know what to ask for.
  • Proverbs 21:5 — “The thoughts of the diligent tend only to plenteousness; but of every one that is hasty only to want.” Diligence here means careful, steady planning, set against hasty decisions made in panic. A written plan, even a rough one, is a way of trusting God with real numbers instead of vague dread.

How do I pray when I'm worried about money?

  1. Ask for daily bread. Jesus taught his disciples to ask God for this day's bread (Matthew 6:11). It's a small, specific, one-day request, and it's the prayer he gave for exactly this kind of worry.
  2. Name the numbers. Tell God the actual amount, the due date, and the worst case you're afraid of. He already knows. Saying it out loud pulls the dread out of the fog and sets it in front of him.
  3. Give thanks for one provision. Before you ask for more, thank him for something he has already supplied this month, however small. It reminds you who you're talking to.
  4. Take one practical step. After you pray, do one thing today: open the statement, call the lender, list your expenses, or book an appointment with a nonprofit credit counselor. Prayer and action belong together.
  5. Tell someone. Shame keeps money trouble secret, and secrecy makes it heavier. The early church shared what they had so that no one among them was in need (Acts 4:34). Asking your church or a trusted friend for help may be part of how God answers your prayer.

Does trusting God mean ignoring financial problems?

No. The same Bible that tells you not to be anxious about tomorrow also sends you to watch the ant, who gathers food in summer for the months ahead (Proverbs 6:6–8). Trust and planning work together. Trust deals with the fear underneath; planning deals with the bills in front of you.

What Scripture warns against is letting panic make the decisions: spending to feel better, leaving the mail unopened, or freezing up entirely. Those tend to make the trouble heavier. It also warns against letting money become the thing you trust most, whether you have a little or a lot. If your money worry comes from losing work, these Bible verses for job loss and unemployment speak to that directly, and these Bible verses for anxiety and worry can help with the worry itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a sin to worry about money?

No. Worry about money is a human response to real pressure, and Jesus spoke about it with patience, not contempt. He treats it as something to bring to the Father, who knows what you need. Worry becomes a spiritual problem when it pushes you toward dishonesty, makes money your main source of security, or keeps you from asking for help.

What does “take no thought” mean in Matthew 6?

In 1611 English, to “take thought” meant to be anxious or troubled. So Jesus isn't forbidding budgets or planning ahead, and modern translations render it “do not worry.” He is saying: don't let fear about food, clothing, and tomorrow rule you, because your Father knows your needs and his kingdom comes first.

Does God promise financial prosperity in the Bible?

God promises to care for his people's needs, not to make every believer wealthy. Jesus had nowhere to lay his head (Matthew 8:20), and Paul knew hunger. Proverbs describe how life generally works under God; they aren't contracts. Be wary of teaching that promises money in return for giving money. Scripture's picture of blessing is enough, gratitude, and generosity.

Should I keep tithing if I can't pay my bills?

Christians hold different views on tithing, so talk honestly with your pastor. Scripture values giving from a willing heart and says it is accepted “according to that a man hath, and not according to that he hath not” (2 Corinthians 8:12). Providing for your household is also a God-given responsibility. A good pastor will want to help you steady things, not add pressure.

How do I trust God when I'm in debt?

Trust in debt looks like honesty, small faithful steps, and help. Be truthful with God and with the people you owe. If the debt is large, make a plan with a qualified counselor. Keep thanking God for what you do have. And remember that your worth isn't measured by your balance; God's care for you doesn't rise and fall with your credit score.
