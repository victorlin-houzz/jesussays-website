Quiet Time With God: A Simple Guide

By the Jesus Says teamAI-assisted, reviewed by VictorUpdated October 24, 2026Scripture: King James Version

Quiet time with God is a regular pause to read Scripture, reflect on what it says, and pray. Begin with a short passage and a few honest minutes, at a time you can usually keep. You can pray in the morning or evening, read or listen, and start again whenever you miss a day.

Begin with a passage, a place, and enough time to listen

You need a Bible and a place where you can give the passage some attention. A chair before the household wakes, a lunch break, or the few minutes after a shift can work. A perfectly silent room is helpful when available, but interruptions do not make your prayer less welcome.

And he withdrew himself into the wilderness, and prayed.

Luke 5:16

In Luke 5:12–16, crowds were coming to Jesus to hear him and seek healing. Jesus still withdrew to pray. His example gives us reason to make room for prayer amid real responsibilities, without turning his particular schedule into a rule for everyone.

Choose the passage before you begin. A Gospel paragraph is a good place to start because you can follow what Jesus says and does. Mark 1:35–39 shows him praying and then returning to his work. Read the whole scene rather than looking for a sentence that seems to predict what will happen today.

A five-minute quiet time you can try today

  1. Settle for a moment. Put other tasks aside where possible. Ask God to help you attend to his word. You do not have to empty your mind or create a particular feeling.
  2. Read Mark 1:35–39 slowly. Notice where Jesus goes, what the disciples want, and how he responds. Read the passage again if the first reading was hurried.
  3. Name one thing the passage shows. Jesus makes time to pray, and he remains attentive to the work before him. Ask what you need to receive or practice, without assuming every detail is an instruction for your day.
  4. Pray about what is actually ahead. Bring a responsibility, a person, or something you are grateful for to God. Use your own words or the prayer below.
  5. Choose one small response. You might make space to pray tomorrow, listen patiently to someone, or finish a task honestly. Carry the passage into ordinary life.

The minutes are a suggestion, not a timer you have to obey. If reading takes longer, stay with it. If you have only a brief pause, read a few lines and pray. For a passage with a ready-written reflection and next step, use the five-minute daily devotional.

Father, help me pay attention to your word today. Show me what the passage says before I rush to what I want it to say.

I bring you the people and responsibilities on my mind: [name them]. Give me wisdom for the next step, patience with interruptions, and a willing heart to do what is right. In Jesus’ name, amen.

Verses for reading, praying, and returning to your day

A familiar verse can help you begin when you are unsure what to read. Keep its surrounding passage close too, so the words remain connected to the writer’s meaning.

  • Psalm 5:3 — “My voice shalt thou hear in the morning, O LORD; in the morning will I direct my prayer unto thee, and will look up.” David brings his request to God with expectation. You can name a real need before the day fills up.
  • Psalm 119:18 — “Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law.” The psalmist asks for understanding. Pray for help to receive what Scripture teaches instead of treating reading as a task to finish.
  • James 1:22 — “But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves.” James connects hearing with action. Let one response to the passage shape how you treat someone today.

The text and study tools for Psalm 119:18 let you read the verse alongside the rest of the psalm. When an unfamiliar word catches your attention, checking its meaning is part of a thoughtful quiet time. You do not need to understand every detail before you can pray.

When you have fifteen minutes

Give the extra time to context before adding more activities. Read what comes before and after your chosen paragraph. Notice who is speaking, who is listening, and what question or situation the words address.

Write two short notes: what the passage says about God, and what response it invites from you. Keep those notes separate from guesses about why a particular event happened in your life. Scripture offers guidance without making every thought that occurs during prayer a message from God.

Leave time to pray for other people as well as yourself. A longer sitting can include gratitude, confession, requests, and silence. The guide to prayer in Matthew 6 explains how the Lord’s Prayer can give that time a shape.

Make room for the way you can participate

Listening to an audio Bible is a valid way to attend to Scripture, including when reading is tiring or difficult. Pause the recording to think or pray if that helps. Quiet instrumental music is optional; keep it low enough to follow the passage, and leave it off if choosing a track becomes a distraction.

When your mind wanders, return gently to the sentence you were reading. You can jot down a task you are afraid of forgetting, then come back to the passage. Some days attention is harder than others. Concentration is not a measure of how much God welcomes you.

After a missed day, begin with the next available moment. You do not owe a double session. A private devotional practice belongs alongside worship, shared prayer, and life with other Christians; it does not replace them.
