A Confession Prayer for Addiction Recovery

By the Jesus Says teamAI-assisted, reviewed by VictorUpdated September 29, 2026Scripture: King James Version

A confession prayer for addiction recovery tells God honestly what you’ve been doing, hiding, or unable to stop, and asks for his mercy and help. Scripture treats confession as a way back to God and toward healing, never as self-punishment. Pray it alongside people who can walk with you: a pastor, a doctor, and a recovery group.

Care note: Prayer and confession support recovery; they don’t replace a doctor, counselor, pastor, or recovery group. Stopping some substances suddenly can be dangerous, so talk with a doctor before you quit. In the US, the SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) is free, confidential, and open day and night. For an overdose or other emergency, call 911 or local emergency services. If you are thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US).

What does the Bible say about confession and recovery?

Scripture is honest about people who are stuck, and it doesn’t leave them there.

  • Psalm 32:5 — “I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid. I said, I will confess my transgressions unto the LORD; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin.” Just before this, David describes what hiding did to him: “my bones waxed old through my roaring all the day long.” Secrecy wears a person down. Telling the truth is where relief starts.
  • James 5:16 — “Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed.” Confession to God comes first, but James asks for more: tell another believer. Addiction grows in hiding and weakens when someone safe knows.
  • Romans 7:15 — “For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I.” Here “allow” means approve. Christians differ on whether Paul describes life before or after faith, but many people in recovery recognize their own struggle in his words.
  • Romans 8:1 — “There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit.” This is Paul’s answer to his cry in Romans 7:24, “who shall deliver me?” A relapse doesn’t cancel your standing in Christ. The closing words point to the new life the Spirit grows in you, which Paul explains through the rest of Romans 8.
  • 1 Corinthians 10:13 — “There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it.” “Suffer” means allow. The way of escape is often ordinary: leaving the room, calling your sponsor, taking a different route home. Plan yours before the craving comes.

How do I pray a confession prayer for addiction?

  1. Come as you are. You don’t have to be clean for a week before you pray. In Jesus’ parable, the father ran to his son “when he was yet a great way off” (Luke 15:20).
  2. Name it plainly. Tell God what you used or did, how often, what you hid, and who was affected. Vague words like I messed up again keep things foggy. Plain words bring them into the light.
  3. Bring what’s underneath. Pain, grief, loneliness, fear, or old wounds often sit beneath the habit. “The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart” (Psalm 34:18). Pray about those too.
  4. Ask for mercy and for help. Ask God to forgive you, and ask him for the next right step, not only for more willpower.
  5. Receive forgiveness. Trust his promise even if shame still clings to you. The confession prayer guide walks through Psalm 51 if you want a fuller pattern.
  6. Tell one person and take one step. Call your pastor, a sponsor, or a trusted friend this week. Then do one concrete thing: book a doctor’s appointment, find a meeting, or remove what you can from your home.

Is prayer enough, or do I need a recovery program?

Most people need both. Addiction involves the body, habits, and relationships as well as the will, so private prayer and willpower usually aren’t enough alone. God often answers prayers for freedom through other people.

Bear ye one another’s burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ.

Galatians 6:2

Help can include a pastor or priest, a Christian recovery ministry such as Celebrate Recovery, a twelve-step group, a licensed counselor, your doctor, or a treatment program. Many twelve-step groups ask members to admit their wrongs to God, to themselves, and to another person, which echoes James 5:16. If a doctor recommends treatment or medication, following that advice is a way of accepting help, not a lack of trust in God.

Does God forgive me if I relapse?

Yes. The writer of Lamentations was sitting in the ruins of Jerusalem when he wrote these words, and he still found reason to hope:

It is of the LORD’S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.

Lamentations 3:22–23

Come back the same day. Don’t wait until you feel you’ve earned it. Tell your support person what happened, look honestly at what led up to it, and add support where the plan was thin. If shame keeps pulling you under afterward, read the confession prayer after failure.

Lord Jesus, I come to you as I am. I have been [name it plainly], and I have hidden it from [name]. I can’t fix this by myself, and I’m tired of pretending. Have mercy on me.

Forgive me for the harm I’ve done to myself and to the people I love. Thank you that there is no condemnation for those who are in you. Give me the courage to tell the truth to someone today, the humility to accept help, and a way of escape when the craving comes.

Heal what lies underneath this, too. When I fall, help me come back quickly. Your mercies are new this morning, and I trust you with today. Amen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is addiction a sin?

Scripture names some behaviors tied to addiction, such as drunkenness, as sin (Galatians 5:19–21), and it calls us to repent of what we choose. Addiction also involves pain, the body, and patterns that are very hard to break, so it needs care as well as repentance. Either way the next step is the same: God welcomes you back, and you don’t have to face it alone.

Who should I confess my addiction to?

Choose someone safe and mature who will respond with truth and grace, not gossip or contempt: a pastor or priest, a sponsor, a counselor, or a trusted Christian friend. Catholics may also bring it to the sacrament of confession. If a spouse or family member has been affected, telling them may be part of making things right, and a pastor or counselor can help you plan that conversation.

How do I pray for someone else’s addiction?

Pray for them by name: for their safety, for honesty, for a moment of clarity, and for the right people to reach them. Pray for yourself too, because loving someone with an addiction is exhausting. Love doesn’t mean covering for them or paying for their habit. Family support groups such as Al-Anon and Nar-Anon, along with your pastor, can help you set wise limits.

Can God take away my cravings instantly?

Some Christians describe sudden freedom, and God can do that. Many others find change comes slowly, one day and one choice at a time, through prayer, people, and treatment. Both are God’s work. Don’t stop a treatment plan or prescribed medication because you have prayed. Talk with your doctor first, and keep your support in place.

What if I’ve confessed the same thing again and again?

Keep coming. When Peter asked how often to forgive, Jesus answered, “Until seventy times seven” (Matthew 18:22). God’s mercy toward you is not smaller than the mercy he asks of us. Confessing the same struggle many times isn’t proof you are beyond help. It is usually a sign that you need more support around you, not less honesty with God.
